## 1. Purpose

To set out the licence the client must hold before it may lawfully import the five product
lines into South Africa, the route and evidence required to obtain it, the classification
questions that must be resolved first, and the decisions the client has to make.

## 2. Executive summary

1. **A licence is required before any stock moves.** Section 22C(6) of the Medicines and
   Related Substances Act 101 of 1965 ("the Act") prohibits importing a medicine unless the
   importer holds a licence under section 22C(1)(b). SAHPRA confirms this applies to
   Category D complementary medicines. `[VERIFY: s22C(6) wording]`
2. **Importing is not the whole activity.** If the client will sell on to retailers,
   pharmacies or online resellers, that is wholesale distribution and requires wholesale /
   distribution authority in addition to import authority. Scope the application to the
   full activity set now; adding it later is a chargeable amendment. **Decision D1.**
3. **Apply through the Category D route, not the general pharmaceutical route.** SAHPRA
   operates a dedicated complementary medicines licensing platform with its own licence
   types (DL01/DL02/DL03) and an **attestation-based** process. `[VERIFY: licence type codes
   and current portal]`
4. **The attestation is a binding commitment, not a formality.** The licence is issued on
   the strength of the applicant's declaration that it already meets minimum standards, and
   is valid five years during which SAHPRA inspects to verify that declaration. Attesting
   to controls that do not exist is the principal risk in this engagement. See §7.
5. **Classification must be settled per product before the schedule is drafted.** Two of
   the five lines (Selenium, Psyllium Husk) carry a real possibility of falling outside
   "health supplement" treatment. Selenium turns on a dose threshold; Psyllium turns on the
   claims made. See §5.
6. **Registration is not currently the gate — the licence is.** No complementary medicine
   has yet been registered in South Africa and the original call-up notices have been
   repealed. The client can trade on an unregistered basis provided it is licensed and
   label-compliant, but must be ready for a future call-up. See §6.
7. **Critical path is client data, then the responsible person, then the SOP set.** None of
   these are quick. Do not give the client a submission date until the §9 decisions are made
   and the information request is returned.

## 4. Which licence to apply for

SAHPRA's Category D licence types are reported as: `[VERIFY all three]`

| Type | Scope | Applies to |
|---|---|---|
| **DL01** | Manufacture, import or export Category D medicines | Manufacturers |
| **DL02** | Import or export Category D medicines | Importers/exporters |
| **DL03** | Act as a wholesaler of, or distribute, Category D medicines | Wholesalers / distributors |

**Working position:** the client is an importer that does not manufacture, so **DL02** is the
primary application, with **DL03** added if the client sells on to trade customers.

**Two cautions before this is treated as settled:**

- One source describes DL02 as being for *"holders of certificate of registration"*. Since
  no complementary medicine has been registered in South Africa, that qualifier cannot be
  read literally, and it may indicate that the licence types are scoped differently in the
  current guideline. **Confirm the correct type directly with SAHPRA Pharma Licensing
  before paying any fee.**
- If any line proves to contain a Schedule 1 or higher substance, the Category D attestation
  route is not the right vehicle for it — the general licensing route (SAHPGL-LIC-01/-02)
  and a responsible pharmacist registered with the South African Pharmacy Council are
  engaged instead. This is a live possibility for Selenium (§5).

## 5. Product classification — the analysis that must precede the schedule

Classification is not a labelling detail; it determines which Act applies, whether a licence
is needed at all, who may sell the product and what may be said about it.

**Why this is sharper than usual.** In *Minister of Health and Another v Alliance of Natural
Health Products (South Africa)* [2022] ZASCA 49 (11 April 2022) the Supreme Court of Appeal
dismissed the appeal by the Minister and SAHPRA, leaving standing the High Court's
declaration that the 2017 General Regulations are unlawful **to the extent that they apply to
"complementary medicines" and "health supplements" that are not "medicines" or "scheduled
substances" as defined in the Act**. The declaration of invalidity was suspended to allow the
defect to be corrected, and the cross-appeal against that suspension was also dismissed.

Practical consequences for this engagement:

- Whether a product is a *medicine* turns substantially on **what is claimed for it and how
  it is presented**, not on what is in the bottle. The same capsule may be a medicine or a
  foodstuff depending on the label.
- A product that is genuinely a foodstuff is regulated by the National Department of Health
  under Act 54 of 1972, cleared through Port Health, and needs no SAHPRA licence.
- **This is not a reason to skip the licence.** SAHPRA continues to administer the Category D
  framework; port-of-entry release of health products is predicated on the importer being
  licensed; and misjudging the boundary leaves the client importing unlicensed medicines.
  The commercial reality is that supplement-type products are marketed with health claims,
  which pulls them into the medicines regime.
- **Status to confirm:** whether the General Regulations have since been amended to cure the
  defect, and SAHPRA's current published stance on the judgment. `[VERIFY — this materially
  affects the advice and must be checked before the memo is issued to the client.]`

### 5.2 Labelling obligations that follow

Whichever products are confirmed as Category D, their labels must comply with General
Regulations 10, 11 and 12, and unregistered complementary medicines must carry the
prescribed disclaimer to the effect that the medicine has not been evaluated by SAHPRA for
its quality, safety or intended use. `[VERIFY exact current wording and placement
requirements before instructing the client's printer — relabelling is expensive.]`

Imported stock frequently arrives with labels compliant in the country of origin but not in
South Africa. Resolve this **before** the first order, not at the port.

## 6. Registration status and what it means commercially

- Complementary medicines are subject to registration under section 14 of the Act, called up
  in phases by call-up notice.
- **As at the most recent reporting (August 2026), no complementary medicine has been
  registered in South Africa and the initial call-up notices have been repealed.** `[VERIFY
  against SAHPRA's current position]`
- Continued rights of sale for complementary medicines have been conditioned on the
  supplier being appropriately licensed under section 22C(1)(b), on registration
  applications being lodged by the deadline of any applicable call-up notice, and on
  compliance with section 20 and the labelling regulations.

**What to tell the client:** the licence is the live obligation; registration is a future
obligation on a timetable SAHPRA has not yet reset. The client should budget for a
registration workstream and keep product dossiers (composition, specifications, stability,
evidence for claims) in order from day one, so that a call-up does not find them unprepared.
Dossier-grade data is far easier to obtain from a manufacturer while commercial negotiations
are open than two years into a supply relationship.

## 7. The attestation route — what the client is signing up to

Category D licences are reported to be issued on SAHPRA's acceptance of the applicant's
**attestation of compliance with minimum requirements**, together with payment of the licence
application and desktop evaluation fees. Licences are valid **five years**, during which the
holder should be **inspected to verify the attestation**. `[VERIFY]`

Minimum standards are met either by compliance with Good Manufacturing Practice evidenced by
a valid GMP certificate, or by providing the full information required in the application,
including Annexure C of SAHPGL-PEM-COMP-02. `[VERIFY Annexure C contents]`

As a minimum the applicant must hold standard operating procedures for:

- quality assurance product release;
- recall;
- finished product specifications and testing;
- determination of shelf life (expiry date).

Where an activity has not yet commenced, SOPs together with the blank record templates are
acceptable; once the activity commences, completed records for at least the preceding twelve
months must be maintained. `[VERIFY]`

> **Risk.** The attestation converts the client's paperwork into a regulatory undertaking.
> An inspection that finds no recall capability, no release procedure and no records behind
> an attestation of compliance puts the licence — and the client's ability to trade — at
> risk. Alpha CR Solutions should not sign or co-sign an attestation it has not tested.
> See `06-qms-sop-register.md`.

## 9. Decisions required

**D1 — Activity scope.** Will the client only import for its own account, or also wholesale
and distribute to trade customers? Determines DL02 alone versus DL02 + DL03.

**D2 — Product range.** Is the licence to cover only these five lines, or the complete range
the line numbers imply? Strong recommendation: licence the full range in one application.

**D3 — Responsible person.** Who is the named responsible person, and are they a pharmacist
registered with the South African Pharmacy Council? Required in the general licensing route;
the position for Category D needs confirmation. Usually the longest lead item — start now.
